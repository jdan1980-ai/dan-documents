## §12 — Community Post (publish day-of or day-before)

```
MONO NO AWARE (物の哀れ) in Japanese Culture: A Concise Overview

MONO NO AWARE (物の哀れ) is one of the oldest and most distinctly Japanese aesthetic principles — the bittersweet awareness that all beautiful things are transient. Coined by the 18th-century scholar Motoori Norinaga, the phrase translates roughly as "the pathos of things" — the tender ache felt when watching cherry blossoms fall, or fireflies rise on the last warm evening of summer. Not grief exactly, but a full-bodied recognition that beauty is inseparable from its passing. In Japanese art, from the waka poems of the Heian court to the films of Ozu and Miyazaki, mono no aware is the emotional key that gives beauty its depth. As the tea ceremony principle 一期一会 (ichi-go ichi-e) reminds us: this encounter will not come again. To feel that — and still remain — is the practice.
```
